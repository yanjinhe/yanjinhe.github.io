SWIFT: Self-guided Weak-to-strong dIFfusion models via Target extrapolation

TL;DR. SWIFT moves autoguidance-style weak-to-strong extrapolation from sampling into training: the denoiser regresses onto a target pushed past the clean sample, away from a frozen weak denoiser's prediction. The weak denoiser is discarded after training, so inference keeps one network and the original sampler. On ImageNet-256, SWIFT reaches FID 1.15 without guidance and 0.99 with guidance, and in one configuration beats the baseline's best FID with roughly half the training compute.

Right: on the matched RAE / DINOv2-B configuration, SWIFT stays ahead of the baseline throughout extended training (dots: raw evaluations; lines: five-point moving averages).

Guidance usually waits until sampling.

Diffusion models are trained with a plain denoising regression, yet many of the strongest results rely on guidance applied only at inference. (Karras et al., 2024) is a clean example: a deliberately worse denoiser — smaller, or trained for less time — defines a direction, and at every sampling step the strong prediction is pushed away from it. It works well, but the weak model has to be kept and run whenever guidance is used, and the trained denoiser itself is left unchanged.

Autoguidance evaluates \(D_{\mathrm{AG}} = (1+s)\,D_{\mathrm{base}} - s\,D_{\mathrm{weak}}\) at every sampling step, where \(s\) is the guidance scale. Classifier-free guidance has the same form, with the unconditional prediction in place of the weak one. Both act on a denoiser whose training is already finished.

This suggests a different role for weak models: not teachers to imitate, but reference points to move away from. Our question was whether this weak-to-strong extrapolation can be moved from inference into training, so that the final denoiser absorbs the correction and the weak model can be thrown away afterwards.

SWIFT: extrapolate the target, not the prediction.

Ordinary training regresses the denoiser \(D_\theta(\bm{x}_t,\sigma_t)\) onto the clean sample \(\bm{x}_0\); the population optimum of this regression is the . SWIFT keeps the loss, the weighting, the architecture and the sampler, and changes only what the denoiser is asked to predict:

With \(\bm{x}_t = \bm{x}_0 + \bm{\varepsilon}_t\), \(\bm{\varepsilon}_t \sim \mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\sigma_t^2\bm{I})\), the minimizer of \(\mathbb{E}\,\lambda(t)\|D(\bm{x}_t,\sigma_t)-\bm{x}_0\|^2\) over all functions is \(D^\star(\bm{x}_t,\sigma_t) = \mathbb{E}[\bm{x}_0 \mid \bm{x}_t]\). Tweedie's formula turns it into the score, \(\nabla_{\bm{x}_t}\log p_t(\bm{x}_t) = (D^\star - \bm{x}_t)/\sigma_t^2\), which is what the sampler integrates. In practice the sampler uses the denoiser reached after finite training, not \(D^\star\).

$$ \begin{aligned} \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SWIFT}}(\theta) &= \mathbb{E}\Big[\lambda(t)\,\big\|D_\theta(\bm{x}_t,\sigma_t) - \bm{y}_\alpha\big\|_2^2\Big],\\ \bm{y}_\alpha &= \bm{x}_0 + \alpha\,\big(\bm{x}_0 - D_{\mathrm{weak}}(\bm{x}_t,\sigma_t)\big). \end{aligned} $$

Here \(D_{\mathrm{weak}}\) is a frozen weak denoiser and \(\alpha \ge 0\) sets the extrapolation strength; \(\alpha = 0\) recovers ordinary training. For \(\alpha \gt 0\) the target lies beyond the clean sample, on the side away from the weak prediction. This is the autoguidance formula with the observed \(\bm{x}_0\) in place of the strong model's prediction, used as a regression target rather than at sampling time. Indeed, conditioned on \(\bm{x}_t\), the target averages to \((1+\alpha)D^\star - \alpha D_{\mathrm{weak}}\): autoguidance applied to the ideal denoiser.

Target extrapolation, in two dimensions. Training starts at 0 and, after a finite budget, stops short of what it aims at (dotted). Aimed at the posterior mean \(D^\star\), it lands at \(D_{\mathrm{weak}}\), off by a residual \(v_1\) that depends on the budget and the architecture. SWIFT aims past \(D^\star\), away from \(D_{\mathrm{weak}}\). Training toward this target leaves a similar residual \(v_2\) (the two differ only by a term proportional to \(\alpha\)), so the learned \(D_{\mathrm{SWIFT}}\) lands closer to \(D^\star\).

The guide is an ordinary denoiser, trained with the standard loss and frozen before the main run. By default it is smaller and trained for less time: on DiffusionBench it has about a quarter of the main model's parameters and is trained for half its standard duration. It runs without gradients, adding about per training step, and is discarded afterwards. The method is self-guided because the correction comes from a weaker denoiser trained on the same task: no external teacher, no separate guidance objective, no auxiliary head, and no label dropout or unconditional branch. The same construction works for conditional and unconditional generation, and velocity-predicting models apply it to the .

The DiffusionBench guide's forward pass costs about 26% of the main model's (87.3 vs. 337.5 GFLOPs per sample). A baseline update is one forward and one backward pass, about three forward-equivalents; SWIFT adds one guide forward pass and no backward pass through it, so an update costs about \(1 + 0.26/3 \approx 1.09\) times the baseline. Pretraining the guide costs about 10.4 baseline-equivalent epochs, paid once and reusable across runs; charged to a single 80-epoch run, the total is about 1.22 times an 80-epoch baseline.

The ImageNet models use linear flow matching, \(\bm{x}_t = (1-t)\,\bm{x}_0 + t\,\bm{\epsilon}\), and most of them predict the velocity \(\bm{v} = \bm{\epsilon} - \bm{x}_0\). At a fixed noisy input, the velocity is affine in the clean prediction, so extrapolating the clean target is the same as extrapolating the velocity target: \(\bm{v}_{\mathrm{SWIFT}} = (1+\alpha)\,\bm{v} - \alpha\,\bm{v}_{\mathrm{weak}}\), with \(\bm{v}_{\mathrm{weak}}\) the frozen guide's prediction.

Keeping the guide frozen is deliberate: for a given clean sample, noise draw and noise level, the target never changes during training. Related training-time methods instead build their correction from .

Model Guidance builds its training correction from the model's own (EMA) conditional and unconditional predictions; Segmented Guidance switches between an unconditional prediction and an intermediate branch across noise levels; Guidance-Free Training trains across guidance strengths with a detached unconditional prediction of the same model; Internal Guidance supervises an intermediate output of the same backbone and uses it for guidance at sampling time. In all of them the reference changes as training proceeds; detaching it within one update does not make it fixed. In our CIFAR-10 comparisons, jointly training the guide gave no clear additional endpoint benefit over keeping it frozen.

What the correction looks like.

On a two-class, tree-shaped 2D distribution with thin branching support (after the fractal example of Karras et al.), SWIFT follows the narrow branches more closely than classifier-free guidance, autoguidance and four other guidance methods, each with independently tuned settings. It has the lowest KL divergence (0.728 vs. 0.817 for GFT, the strongest baseline), the lowest outlier rate (3.66% vs. 4.97%) and the highest log-likelihood (+0.08 vs. −0.40).

Why a deliberately biased target can give a better denoiser.

This may look suspicious. The population optimum of the standard loss is the posterior mean \(D^\star\), and the SWIFT target is biased away from it: with unlimited capacity and training, SWIFT would converge to \((1+\alpha)D^\star - \alpha D_{\mathrm{weak}}\) and miss \(D^\star\) by \(\alpha\,(D^\star - D_{\mathrm{weak}})\).

The catch is that training stops long before that point. After a finite budget the baseline has learned only part of \(D^\star\), and an earlier, weaker checkpoint has learned less of the same directions. Moving the target away from that checkpoint amplifies the components that are still underfit, and the main model absorbs part of this extra signal within the same budget.

We make this precise when training is modeled as , for any data distribution with finite second moment. On a learnable kernel mode with eigenvalue \(\kappa\), the baseline trained for time \(T\) still misses a fraction \(e^{-T\kappa}\) of \(D^\star\), while SWIFT adds a correction \(\alpha\,(1-e^{-T\kappa})\,e^{-T_g\kappa}\), where \(T_g \lt T\) is when the guide was frozen. The two terms have opposite signs, so a small positive \(\alpha\) partially cancels the shortfall: there is an \(\alpha^\star \gt 0\) such that every \(0 \lt \alpha \lt \alpha^\star\) gives strictly lower error to the posterior mean than matched standard training (Theorem 1 in the paper). A larger \(\alpha\) can overshoot, consistent with the U-shape we see in practice.

Write \(L\) for the kernel integral operator on \(L^2(P_{\bm{x}_t})\) and \(H_s = I - e^{-sL}\) for the response of zero-initialized gradient flow after time \(s\). Then \(D_{\mathrm{weak}} = H_{T_g}D^\star\), \(D_{\mathrm{base}} = H_T D^\star\) and \(D^{(\alpha)}_{\mathrm{SWIFT}} = H_T\big[(1+\alpha)D^\star - \alpha D_{\mathrm{weak}}\big]\). On a kernel mode with eigenvalue \(\kappa \gt 0\), the error of \(D^{(\alpha)}_{\mathrm{SWIFT}}\) is

$$ \Big(\underbrace{-\,e^{-T\kappa}}_{\text{finite-time residual}} \;+\; \underbrace{\alpha\,(1-e^{-T\kappa})\,e^{-T_g\kappa}}_{\text{target correction}}\Big)\, d^\star_\kappa , $$

where \(d^\star_\kappa\) is the coefficient of \(D^\star\) on that mode; directions the kernel cannot learn receive no correction. The result is about population denoising MSE at one noise level and equal main-model training time; guide preparation is not counted, and it does not by itself imply a better FID. The paper's appendix also treats a smaller, spectrally nested guide, finite-step training and finite samples.

Note what the guide does not need to be: good. In the theorem the guide has strictly larger error than the baseline. SWIFT does not learn from a better model; it learns away from a worse one.

Results on ImageNet-256.

On ImageNet, SWIFT uses one fixed recipe: \(\alpha = 0.4\) and a smaller pretrained guide, frozen before main-model training. Across 18 DiffusionBench configurations, spanning VAE latents, representation-autoencoder latents, end-to-end learned latents and pixel space, this recipe lowers unguided FID in every case, by 16–49% (median 31%). At the best available guidance settings it lowers guided FID in all 18 as well. On the stronger RAEv2 model, SWIFT reaches FID 1.15 without guidance and 0.99 with guidance.

Complementary to inference-time guidance.

SWIFT absorbs part of the weak-to-strong correction, not all of it. Inference-time guidance — autoguidance on the DiffusionBench models, native internal guidance on RAEv2 — still improves a SWIFT model, and SWIFT plus independently retuned guidance gives the best FID in all five configurations we tested. The preferred amount of guidance also moves: on RAEv2 the best internal-guidance scale drops from 1.78 for the baseline to 1.20 for SWIFT, while the best guided FID improves from 1.04 to 0.99. This is consistent with the training-time and inference-time corrections overlapping in part, with the rest still complementary.

SWIFT with inference-time guidance. (a) FID with autoguidance on the DiffusionBench models and internal guidance on RAEv2. (b) On RAEv2, SWIFT shifts the preferred internal-guidance scale from 1.78 to 1.20.

Training cost, and the gain over longer training.

SWIFT adds about 9% FLOPs per epoch for the frozen guide, plus roughly ten baseline-equivalent epochs to pretrain it. Charging both, SWIFT on DINOv2-B passes the best FID the baseline reaches in 320 epochs (1.53) already at epoch 120 (1.50), using about 0.45× the training FLOPs. On RAEv2, epoch-50 SWIFT beats the epoch-160 baseline (1.34 vs. 1.56) at 0.44× the FLOPs, guide pretraining included. These ratios are conservative: within the observed training horizon, the baselines never reach SWIFT's best FID at all.

The gap does not close with more training either. Continuing the DINOv2-B pair to 240 epochs, SWIFT leads at all 36 evaluations and the margin grows from 0.12 to 0.26 FID (1.63 for the baseline vs. 1.38 for SWIFT at epoch 240). Across the 12 DiffusionBench configurations whose baselines were also tracked during training, SWIFT is ahead at every evaluation from epoch 40 on.

How to choose \(\alpha\) and the weak guide.

CIFAR-10 ablations. (a) The coefficient \(\alpha\), with a half-width guide. (b) The guide's width, with the student at channel base 128. (c) How long the guide is trained before it is frozen.

  • The coefficient is broadly U-shaped. On CIFAR-10 the favorable region is around \(\alpha = 0.7\)–\(0.9\); on ImageNet (DINOv2-B), \(\alpha \in [0.5, 1.0]\) substantially improves over the baseline. The optimum depends on the model, so all breadth experiments use \(\alpha = 0.4\) without tuning; a small sweep over moderate positive values is enough. Negative \(\alpha\) speeds up the early drop in FID but gives no consistent final advantage.
  • The guide should be weaker than the student, but competent. At \(\alpha = 0.8\) on CIFAR-10, halving the guide's width (channel base 128 → 64) improves FID from 2.58 to 1.60, while much smaller guides degrade quickly. Half width is the best width across the tested \(\alpha\) on CIFAR-10, and it beats wider guides on ImageNet.
  • Past a competence threshold, the guide's training length matters little. On ImageNet, a guide frozen earlier even did better than the default one in our re-evaluation.
  • A simple fixed recipe is enough. Co-training the guide gave no clear additional benefit. Some capacity switches and coefficient schedules refine CIFAR-10 results while others lose the gain, so the default stays a frozen half-width guide with a fixed, moderate \(\alpha\).

Future directions.

  • The regression target as a design interface. SWIFT writes one correction into the target; what other useful corrections can be written there?
  • Choosing the weak reference. Beyond “smaller and trained for less time”, what makes a good guide? The theory covers earlier checkpoints and spectrally nested smaller guides; a sharper characterization could give better guides at the same cost.
  • Beyond images. SWIFT only changes the regression target, so in principle it applies to other diffusion settings such as video, audio and continuous diffusion language models; we have not tested these yet.
  • From denoising error to sample quality. The theory concerns posterior-mean error at a fixed noise level; connecting it to FID, and extending it to finite samples and nonlinear training dynamics, remains open.
