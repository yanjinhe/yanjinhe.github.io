SWIFT: Self-guided Weak-to-strong dIFfusion models via Target extrapolation

TL;DR. A diffusion model trained for a finite budget falls short of the posterior mean, the optimum of its regression, and models of the same kind trained the same way fall short in the same direction. SWIFT reads that direction off a frozen weak model and pushes the regression target past the clean sample to compensate, so the trained denoiser lands closer to the posterior mean. Inference is unchanged: one network, the original sampler. On ImageNet-256, SWIFT reaches FID 1.15 without guidance and 0.99 with guidance.

Right: on the matched RAE / DINOv2-B configuration, SWIFT stays ahead of the baseline throughout extended training (dots: raw evaluations; lines: five-point moving averages).

Finite training misses the posterior mean, in a predictable direction.

A diffusion model is trained by regressing its denoiser \(D_\theta(\bm{x}_t,\sigma_t)\) onto the clean sample \(\bm{x}_0\). The optimum of this regression is the \(D^\star = \mathbb{E}[\bm{x}_0 \mid \bm{x}_t]\), and the sampler uses the trained denoiser as if it were \(D^\star\). But training stops after a finite budget, so the denoiser we sample with never quite reaches \(D^\star\), and the gap is felt at every sampling step.

With \(\bm{x}_t = \bm{x}_0 + \bm{\varepsilon}_t\), \(\bm{\varepsilon}_t \sim \mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\sigma_t^2\bm{I})\), the minimizer of \(\mathbb{E}\,\lambda(t)\|D(\bm{x}_t,\sigma_t)-\bm{x}_0\|^2\) over all functions is \(D^\star(\bm{x}_t,\sigma_t) = \mathbb{E}[\bm{x}_0 \mid \bm{x}_t]\). Tweedie's formula turns it into the score, \(\nabla_{\bm{x}_t}\log p_t(\bm{x}_t) = (D^\star - \bm{x}_t)/\sigma_t^2\), which is what the sampler integrates.

The gap is not random. A model of a given kind, trained with a given procedure, learns some parts of \(D^\star\) quickly and others slowly, and stops short on the slow ones. Another model of the same kind, trained the same way, stops short on the same parts: models of the same family trained the same way are biased in the same direction relative to their training target. A weaker such model, smaller or stopped earlier, is biased further along that direction, so its prediction shows which way training is going to miss.

That suggests a simple fix. If we know in which direction training falls short, we can aim past the target in the opposite direction, so that the shortfall lands the model near \(D^\star\) rather than short of it.

SWIFT: aim past the target.

SWIFT keeps the loss, the weighting, the architecture and the sampler, and changes only what the denoiser is asked to predict:

$$ \begin{aligned} \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SWIFT}}(\theta) &= \mathbb{E}\Big[\lambda(t)\,\big\|D_\theta(\bm{x}_t,\sigma_t) - \bm{y}_\alpha\big\|_2^2\Big],\\ \bm{y}_\alpha &= \bm{x}_0 + \alpha\,\big(\bm{x}_0 - D_{\mathrm{weak}}(\bm{x}_t,\sigma_t)\big). \end{aligned} $$

Here \(D_{\mathrm{weak}}\) is a frozen weak denoiser and \(\alpha \ge 0\) sets the extrapolation strength; \(\alpha = 0\) recovers ordinary training. The target is pushed past the clean sample, away from the weak prediction, that is, against the direction in which a model like ours falls short. Conditioned on \(\bm{x}_t\), it averages to \((1+\alpha)D^\star - \alpha D_{\mathrm{weak}}\).

Target extrapolation, in two dimensions. Training starts at 0 and stops short of what it aims at (dotted). Aimed at the posterior mean \(D^\star\), it lands at \(D_{\mathrm{weak}}\), off by \(v_1\). SWIFT aims past \(D^\star\), away from \(D_{\mathrm{weak}}\). Because the same kind of model is trained the same way, the shortfall \(v_2\) toward the new target is close to \(v_1\) (in a linear model of training they differ only by a term proportional to \(\alpha\)), so the learned \(D_{\mathrm{SWIFT}}\) lands much closer to \(D^\star\).

The guide is an ordinary denoiser trained with the standard loss, for example an early checkpoint of ordinary training, and frozen before the main run. In our default ImageNet setup it is smaller, with about a quarter of the main model's parameters, and trained for half as long. It runs without gradients, adding about per training step, and is thrown away after training, so sampling uses one network and the original sampler. The method is self-guided: the correction comes from a weaker denoiser on the same task, with no external teacher, no auxiliary head and no label dropout. It works for conditional and unconditional generation, and velocity-predicting models apply it to the .

The DiffusionBench guide's forward pass costs about 26% of the main model's (87.3 vs. 337.5 GFLOPs per sample). A baseline update is one forward and one backward pass, about three forward-equivalents; SWIFT adds one guide forward pass and no backward pass through it, so an update costs about \(1 + 0.26/3 \approx 1.09\) times the baseline. Pretraining the guide costs about 10.4 baseline-equivalent epochs, paid once and reusable across runs; charged to a single 80-epoch run, the total is about 1.22 times an 80-epoch baseline.

The ImageNet models use linear flow matching, \(\bm{x}_t = (1-t)\,\bm{x}_0 + t\,\bm{\epsilon}\), and most of them predict the velocity \(\bm{v} = \bm{\epsilon} - \bm{x}_0\). At a fixed noisy input, the velocity is affine in the clean prediction, so extrapolating the clean target is the same as extrapolating the velocity target: \(\bm{v}_{\mathrm{SWIFT}} = (1+\alpha)\,\bm{v} - \alpha\,\bm{v}_{\mathrm{weak}}\), with \(\bm{v}_{\mathrm{weak}}\) the frozen guide's prediction.

The guide stays frozen, so for a given clean sample, noise draw and noise level the target never changes during training. This sets SWIFT apart from training-time guidance methods whose reference . The same algebraic form also appears in (Karras et al., 2024), which extrapolates a strong model's prediction away from a weak one's at every sampling step; SWIFT arrives at it from the training side, as a correction of where finite training lands, and needs nothing extra at inference.

Model Guidance builds its training correction from the model's own (EMA) conditional and unconditional predictions; Segmented Guidance switches between an unconditional prediction and an intermediate branch across noise levels; Guidance-Free Training trains across guidance strengths with a detached unconditional prediction of the same model; Internal Guidance supervises an intermediate output of the same backbone and uses it for guidance at sampling time. In all of them the reference changes as training proceeds; detaching it within one update does not make it fixed. In our CIFAR-10 comparisons, jointly training the guide gave no clear additional endpoint benefit over keeping it frozen.

Autoguidance evaluates \(D_{\mathrm{AG}} = (1+s)\,D_{\mathrm{base}} - s\,D_{\mathrm{weak}}\) at every sampling step, where \(s\) is the guidance scale. The mean of the SWIFT target, \((1+\alpha)D^\star - \alpha D_{\mathrm{weak}}\), has the same form with the ideal denoiser \(D^\star\) in place of \(D_{\mathrm{base}}\); SWIFT uses it as a regression target instead of a sampling rule.

What the correction looks like.

On a two-class, tree-shaped 2D distribution with thin branching support (after the fractal example of Karras et al.), SWIFT follows the narrow branches more closely than classifier-free guidance, autoguidance and four other guidance methods, each with independently tuned settings. It has the lowest KL divergence (0.728 vs. 0.817 for GFT, the strongest baseline), the lowest outlier rate (3.66% vs. 4.97%) and the highest log-likelihood (+0.08 vs. −0.40).

Why a deliberately biased target gives a better denoiser.

The SWIFT target is biased on purpose: its mean is \((1+\alpha)D^\star - \alpha D_{\mathrm{weak}}\), not \(D^\star\). With unlimited capacity and training, SWIFT would converge to that point and end up worse than ordinary training. So the benefit can only come from finite training, which is the regime every real model is in.

To see it exactly, we model training as , for any data distribution with finite second moment. Split \(D^\star\) along the eigen-directions of the kernel. After training for time \(T\), a direction with eigenvalue \(\kappa\) is learned only up to a fraction \(1-e^{-T\kappa}\): fast directions are almost done, slow ones are not. Ordinary training therefore misses \(D^\star\) by a fraction \(e^{-T\kappa}\) along each direction.

Now take the guide to be the same training stopped earlier, at \(T_g \lt T\). It misses by \(e^{-T_g\kappa}\), more than the baseline and along the same directions: the same-direction bias from above. The extrapolation \(\alpha\,(D^\star - D_{\mathrm{weak}})\) is therefore largest exactly where training is still short. Trained toward the SWIFT target for the same time \(T\), the model's error along each direction becomes

$$ \Big(\underbrace{-\,e^{-T\kappa}}_{\text{finite-time shortfall}} \;+\; \underbrace{\alpha\,(1-e^{-T\kappa})\,e^{-T_g\kappa}}_{\text{SWIFT correction}}\Big)\, d^\star_\kappa , $$

where \(d^\star_\kappa\) is the component of \(D^\star\) along that direction. The correction has the opposite sign of the shortfall in every learnable direction, so a small \(\alpha \gt 0\) shrinks the error in all of them at once. This is Theorem 1 of the paper: there is an \(\alpha^\star \gt 0\) such that every \(0 \lt \alpha \lt \alpha^\star\) gets strictly closer to the posterior mean than standard training with the same budget. Too large an \(\alpha\) overshoots, first along the fast directions, which need the least correction; this is consistent with the U-shape in \(\alpha\) we see in experiments.

Write \(L\) for the kernel integral operator on \(L^2(P_{\bm{x}_t})\) and \(H_s = I - e^{-sL}\) for the response of zero-initialized gradient flow after time \(s\). Then \(D_{\mathrm{weak}} = H_{T_g}D^\star\), \(D_{\mathrm{base}} = H_T D^\star\) and \(D^{(\alpha)}_{\mathrm{SWIFT}} = H_T\big[(1+\alpha)D^\star - \alpha D_{\mathrm{weak}}\big]\). Along an eigen-direction of \(L\) with eigenvalue \(\kappa\), \(H_s\) multiplies by \(1-e^{-s\kappa}\), which gives the formula above; directions the kernel cannot learn receive no correction. The paper's appendix also treats a smaller, spectrally nested guide, finite-step training and finite samples.

Two remarks. The guide does not need to be good: in the theorem it is strictly worse than the baseline, and it only has to be wrong in the same directions. And the result concerns distance to the posterior mean at one noise level, with the guide's own training not counted; it does not by itself guarantee a better FID, which is what the experiments test.

Results on ImageNet-256.

On ImageNet, SWIFT uses one fixed recipe: \(\alpha = 0.4\) and a smaller pretrained guide, frozen before main-model training. Across 18 DiffusionBench configurations, spanning VAE latents, representation-autoencoder latents, end-to-end learned latents and pixel space, this recipe lowers unguided FID in every case, by 16–49% (median 31%), and at the best available guidance settings it lowers guided FID in all 18 as well. On the stronger RAEv2 model, SWIFT reaches FID 1.15 without guidance and 0.99 with guidance.

SWIFT stacks with inference-time guidance.

Guidance at sampling time still helps a SWIFT model: with autoguidance on the DiffusionBench models and internal guidance on RAEv2, SWIFT plus retuned guidance gives the best FID in all five configurations. It also needs less of it: on RAEv2 the best guidance scale drops from 1.78 to 1.20, while guided FID improves from 1.04 to 0.99.

SWIFT with inference-time guidance. (a) FID with autoguidance on the DiffusionBench models and internal guidance on RAEv2. (b) On RAEv2, SWIFT shifts the preferred internal-guidance scale from 1.78 to 1.20.

Training cost, and longer training.

SWIFT adds about 9% FLOPs per training step for the frozen guide, plus about ten baseline-equivalent epochs to pretrain it. Counting both, on DINOv2-B it beats the baseline's best FID over 320 epochs (1.53) by epoch 120 (1.50), with 0.45× the training FLOPs; on RAEv2, epoch-50 SWIFT beats the epoch-160 baseline at 0.44×. The gap does not close with longer training: on DINOv2-B, SWIFT leads at every evaluation up to epoch 240, and in all 12 DiffusionBench configurations tracked during training it leads from epoch 40 on.

How to choose \(\alpha\) and the guide.

CIFAR-10 ablations. (a) The coefficient \(\alpha\), with a half-width guide. (b) The guide's width, with the student at channel base 128. (c) How long the guide is trained before it is frozen.

  • \(\alpha\): moderate positive values work, and the curve is U-shaped (best around 0.7–0.9 on CIFAR-10; 0.5–1.0 works well on ImageNet). All ImageNet results use \(\alpha = 0.4\) without tuning.
  • Guide: weaker than the student, but competent. A half-width guide works best (CIFAR-10 FID 2.58 → 1.60 compared with a full-width one); much smaller guides hurt, and past a competence threshold the guide's training length matters little.
  • Keep it simple: a frozen guide and a fixed \(\alpha\). Co-training the guide gave no clear additional benefit.
